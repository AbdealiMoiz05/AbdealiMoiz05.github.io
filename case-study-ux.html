Business analysis case study · Decent Balloons

Improving the storefront when the platform will not let you

The website was built on NopCommerce, which could not be changed through its admin panel. Every improvement had a licence cost attached, and replatforming meant spending the budget twice. I found a third option.

01 — The constraint

A platform that could not be edited

Decent Balloons ran its online store on NopCommerce. Compared with Shopify or the larger hosted platforms, it is rigid. The admin panel let you manage products and orders. It did not let you meaningfully change how the storefront looked or behaved.

Anything beyond that meant buying a theme, buying a plugin, or paying a developer. On a small family business with no digital budget, each of those was a real cost against uncertain return.

So the store looked and worked the way it had on day one, while the business around it had grown considerably.

The problem was not that we did not know what to improve. It was that the platform charged us for the privilege of improving anything.
02 — Options analysis

Three ways forward

Rather than argue for a budget, I set out the options with their real costs and risks so the owner could decide on evidence.

Why option 3 won. Options 1 and 2 both spent money to buy flexibility. Option 3 bought the same flexibility with skill I already had. It also kept option 1 open: nothing I built made a future migration harder, so replatforming remains available to the business whenever the case for it is strong enough.

Benchmarked against sites that worked

I studied e-commerce stores I thought performed well and worked backwards from what they did that we did not: how products were presented, how someone moved from browsing to enquiry, what the page gave you before you had to ask.

Separated what needed money from what needed effort

A large share of what I wanted turned out to be achievable in the templates and styling rather than through a paid plugin. Establishing that was the analysis. Everything after it was execution.

Modified the storefront directly

Where the admin panel could not reach, I worked in the code. Layout, presentation and the on-page elements that shape how a visitor moves through a catalogue of 5,000 products.

Used SQL against the catalogue

Restructuring how product data was queried and grouped so that the storefront could present it in ways the stock admin interface never intended.

Used AI as a development accelerator

I am not a full-time developer. AI tooling let me work faster on the parts of the implementation that were beyond my day-to-day fluency, while I kept the decisions about what to build.

Shipped in stages, not in one release

Each change went live on its own so its effect could be observed. On a live storefront that also kept the downside of any single change small.

05 — Outcome

Results

Engagement rose by around 60% against the Google Analytics baseline. Visitors were going further into the site rather than leaving from the page they landed on.

No licence or agency spend. The whole improvement came out of skill rather than budget, which mattered on a business of this size.

The migration option stayed open. Nothing I built made moving platform harder. The business chose to stay on NopCommerce because the in-house work removed the reason to spend on a rebuild.

On the numbers. The 60% figure came from Google Analytics, comparing the periods before and after the changes. It was an observed before-and-after rather than a controlled test, so other factors over that period will have contributed. I would isolate individual changes and measure them as experiments if I ran this again.
06 — Reflection

What this taught me

Constraints are the interesting part. With an open budget this is a procurement exercise. With no budget it becomes an analysis problem, and the analysis is where the value was.

Present options, not requests. Asking the owner for money would probably have failed. Setting out three options with costs and risks meant the decision was his and it was made on evidence.

Do not close doors you might need. The in-house work was the right answer at the time, and it was deliberately built so that replatforming stayed possible later.

None of this was in my job description. I took it on because I was invested in the business and I wanted to see whether it could be done. That is still the kind of problem I want to work on.
