Reading customer orders so the order desk doesn't have to

Car makers send purchase orders in whatever format suits them. Someone types each one in and checks it by hand. I built a pipeline that reads them with rules and AI, checks every line against master data, and sends only the doubtful ones to a person in Teams.

01 — Problem

Every customer orders differently

A parts supplier selling to UK vehicle makers gets orders as email tables, chatty emails, PDF purchase orders, pasted spreadsheet rows, and email threads where the buyer corrects a quantity two messages later.

Each order has to be read, keyed into the ERP and checked: is the part number real, is the price the contract price, is the quantity a whole number of packs, can we actually make the date? Doing this by hand is slow, and mistakes don't show up until much later, as a credit note, a re-invoice or a payment held in dispute.

The question I set out to answer: which orders can be booked safely without a person, which ones genuinely need one, and how much time does that free up?

03 — What I built

Six steps, each one a small Python script

Synthetic data with an answer key

Five fictional vehicle makers, a 20-part catalogue with contract discounts, pack sizes and lead times, and 41 orders written in each customer's own format, including real PDFs. I planted 13 known problems so accuracy could be measured, not guessed.

Reading: rules first, AI where it's needed

A free rules reader handles the tidy formats and marks anything it can't handle safely, such as a correction later in a thread, as low confidence. Those orders go to the Claude API, which reads the email or PDF and must return JSON matching a fixed schema. Unreadable fields come back empty and listed, never guessed.

Nine checks in SQLite

Missing PO, unknown customer, unknown part (with the nearest catalogue match), contract price, pack size, lead time, unusual quantity, duplicate PO, and reading confidence.

Routing with a person in the loop

Clean orders are approved. Anything else goes to a review queue with a suggested fix, such as "round to 750 or 700 and confirm with the buyer" or "offer 21 December, or escalate for an expedite".

Teams and SharePoint

Each flagged order becomes a Microsoft Teams Adaptive Card, plus an Excel table that a Power Automate flow loads into a SharePoint review list with an owner and a status.

Measurement and time saved

An evaluation script scores the run against the answer key, and a model turns the measured review rate into hours saved, with every assumption in one editable file.

The finding that shaped the design

All 8 orders the rules reader got wrong came from one customer whose buyer corrects quantities in a later email. The reader couldn't follow the correction, but it noticed one was there and sent the order to a person rather than booking the wrong number. So the cost of the rules approach is review time, not bad orders.

That pointed to where AI is worth paying for: not every order, only the 20% the cheap method can't handle. The pipeline's hybrid mode does exactly that.

Manual time includes 45 minutes of rework for each keying mistake that reaches a customer (3% of orders). Automated time includes 6 minutes per flagged order and a 1-minute spot check on approved ones.

The synthetic inbox has far more problems in it than a real one, so the review rate here (44%) is high. On a real team, the first step would be to time 20 orders by hand and replace the assumptions.
06 — Decisions

Why it's built this way

The AI reads; Python checks.

Business rules belong in code that can be tested and audited, not in a prompt.

A person in the loop.

The system never edits an order. It explains what's wrong and suggests a fix.

Measure, don't claim.

The answer key turns "it works" into a number, and shows exactly where it doesn't.

Cheapest tool first.

Rules handled 80% of orders for free. The AI is paid for only on the rest, and it logs its token cost.

07 — Limits

What this doesn't prove yet

  • The data is synthetic, and I wrote the rules reader knowing the five formats, so real accuracy would be lower.
  • The AI step is built and tested against a mock response. Its accuracy on these orders still has to be measured with a live API key.
  • The Power Automate, Teams and SharePoint flows are designed with a step-by-step build guide. They need a Microsoft 365 account to run.
  • Duplicate checks look within one run. A real version would check against orders already in the ERP.
