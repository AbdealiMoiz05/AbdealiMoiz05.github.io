Business analysis case study · Decent Balloons

One stock pool, five channels, two weeks to restock

A party and events retailer selling 5,000+ products across five channels while running an events business off the same inventory. Nobody could say what was actually available to sell. I designed and built the system that fixed it.

01 — Context

The business

Decent Balloons is a party and events retailer with a catalogue of more than 5,000 products. It sells through five routes: the physical store, its NopCommerce website, Instagram, WhatsApp and Google Business. It also runs an events arm that consumes the same stock to deliver parties and functions.

Restocking ran on a roughly two-week cycle. So a stockout was not a same-day inconvenience. It was a two-week hole.

My role. E-Commerce and Operations Manager, leading a team of five to six people covering e-commerce and direct call sales. Fixing this was not in my job description. I took it on because I could see the same failure happening every fortnight and I wanted to build the thing that stopped it.

02 — Problem definition

The real problem was not stock. It was visibility.

The business believed it had a stock problem. What it had was five systems disagreeing about the same number.

Root cause

Six demand streams were drawing down one physical stock pool, and only one of them, the website, held a stock number at all. The events arm reserved stock without recording it anywhere the sales team could see.

The consequence was that no one could answer "can we sell this today?" without physically walking to the shelf. On a 5,000-SKU catalogue, that check was the bottleneck. It sat in the middle of every order, on every channel, every day.

The two-week restock cycle turned a visibility problem into a revenue problem. Promise something you do not have, and the customer waits a fortnight or cancels.

06 — Solution

What I designed and built

An integrated stock control layer sitting between the physical inventory and every channel that draws on it.

How it works

Single master register

Every product, SKU, quantity, location and minimum stock level held in one place rather than five.

Movement in one channel updates every channel

A sale in store, an order on the website, or an event reservation all draw down the same figure. The number the sales team sees is the number that exists.

Events treated as a demand stream, not an exception

The events arm's reservations became visible stock commitments rather than an informal understanding, so nothing was sold twice.

Reorder points tied to the 14-day cycle

Minimum stock levels set against the real restock lead time, so the reorder trigger fired early enough to matter.

Built in-house, deliberately

I proposed doing this internally rather than bringing in an external supplier. I understood how the business actually ran, and a bought system would have been configured around assumptions rather than the real process.

07 — Change management

The hardest part was not the build

The system worked in week one. Getting it used took the rest of the two months.

The resistance came from the most experienced people in the business, staff in their sixties who had run this operation successfully for years without any of it. Their objection was not unreasonable. They had a working method, they were being asked to replace it with something unfamiliar, and the person asking was considerably younger than them.

What did not work

Explaining the system. Demonstrating the system. Pointing at the stockouts it would have prevented. None of that moved anyone, because none of it answered the question they were actually asking, which was "why should I change something that already works for me?"

What did work

Two things. First, I stopped talking about the technology and started talking about their day: the calls they would not have to make, the trips to the shelf they would not have to take, the customer conversations they would not have to reopen. The benefit had to be theirs, not the business's.

Second, I made the change small. They did not have to learn a system. They had to look at one number before promising stock. Everything else stayed the same at first, and the rest followed once they trusted that number.

Adoption is not a communications problem. It is a design problem. If the first step is hard, nobody takes it.
08 — Outcome

Results

What changed in practice

Order-processing delay fell by around 20%. The manual stock check that sat in the middle of every order was removed.

Channels stopped disagreeing. An order taken on WhatsApp was worked the same way as one taken in store, against the same stock figure.

Restocking got better inputs. Reorder decisions were made against real consumption across all six demand streams rather than one channel's view.

The change survived. The team that resisted it kept using it, which is the outcome most process changes fail to reach.

On the numbers. This was a small family business and improvement was tracked informally rather than through a formal measurement framework. The 20% figure was the business's own assessment of the change over the following trading period. I would set a baseline and a defined metric before starting if I ran this again, and that is the main thing I would do differently.
09 — Reflection

What I took from it

The stated problem is rarely the real one. The business thought it had a stock problem and a staffing problem. It had a data visibility problem that produced both.

Measure first. I improved something I had not properly baselined. The result was real but I cannot describe it as precisely as I would like, and that is a lesson I only get to learn once.

The stakeholder work is the work. I could have built the same system in a fortnight. It took two months because building it was the easy half.

This is why I want to be a business analyst. The part I enjoyed was not the Excel or the Python. It was working out why the process failed, and then getting people to accept a different one.
